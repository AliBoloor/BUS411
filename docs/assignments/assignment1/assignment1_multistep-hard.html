Assignment 1 - Multistep Hard Problem Set

0.1 Instructions

This problem set is based on Lectures 1-6. It contains 30 hard, multi-step questions. Each lecture contributes five questions. The questions use original scenarios and data designed in the style of professional fixed-income practice problems. They are intended to teach the reasoning process, not only test final formulas.

For each question:

  1. Read the scenario carefully.
  2. Identify the cash flows, risks, or market conventions involved.
  3. Work through each part in order.
  4. Compare your reasoning with the full solution.

1 Lecture 1: Bond Features, Structures, Markets, and Risks

1.1 Question 1

A private wealth client is considering three newly issued securities. The client understands stock investing but is new to bonds. You are asked to explain the cash-flow mechanics and risk profile before the client chooses one.

Security Face value Coupon structure Maturity Embedded feature Other information
A USD 1,000 5.20 percent fixed annual coupon 8 years None Bullet repayment at maturity
B USD 1,000 3-month SOFR + 1.40 percent, quarterly reset 5 years Coupon cap at 7.00 percent Bullet repayment at maturity
C USD 1,000 6.00 percent fixed annual coupon 10 years Callable at 102 after year 4 Issuer is expected to refinance if rates decline

Answer the following:

  1. For each security, describe the promised cash-flow pattern before considering default or option exercise.
  2. Identify which security has the most predictable nominal cash flows and explain why.
  3. Identify which security gives the issuer an economically valuable option and explain when that option is most likely to be used.
  4. Explain why Security B may trade close to par after a reset, but still may not be risk-free.
  5. Rank the securities from lowest to highest cash-flow uncertainty for the investor.

Answer.

  1. Security A is a plain fixed-rate bullet bond. It pays USD 52 each year and repays USD 1,000 at maturity. Security B is a floater. Its quarterly coupon changes at each reset based on 3-month SOFR plus 1.40 percent, subject to the 7.00 percent cap, and it repays USD 1,000 at maturity. Security C pays USD 60 each year until maturity unless the issuer calls it after year 4. If called at 102, the investor receives USD 1,020 instead of continuing to receive coupons through year 10.

  2. Security A has the most predictable nominal cash flows. The coupon amount and maturity value are fixed, and there is no embedded option. Security C has fixed coupons too, but the call provision makes the timing of principal repayment uncertain. Security B has uncertain coupons because SOFR changes through time.

  3. Security C gives the issuer the valuable option. The call provision is valuable when market interest rates fall or the issuer’s credit spread tightens enough that it can refinance at a lower cost. In that case, the issuer can redeem the high-coupon bond and replace it with cheaper debt. The investor loses the opportunity to keep earning the 6.00 percent coupon for the full 10 years.

  4. Security B can trade close to par after a reset because the coupon is updated to current short-term rates. If the reference rate resets to market, the coupon is not stale in the way a fixed coupon can be. However, it is not risk-free. Its price can fall if the issuer’s credit spread widens, if liquidity deteriorates, if the coupon cap becomes binding, or if investors require more compensation for the same floater structure.

  5. Lowest uncertainty is Security A because promised nominal cash flows are fixed and bullet-style. Security B is next because the principal timing is fixed but coupons float. Security C has high cash-flow uncertainty because the issuer can change the maturity profile through the call option. If rates fall substantially, the investor should expect early redemption.

1.2 Question 2

A junior analyst summarizes bond risks for an investment committee. The committee asks the analyst to classify each event below using the fixed-income risk categories from Lecture 1.

Event Description
1 Market Treasury yields rise by 90 bps and the price of a fixed-rate bond falls.
2 A BBB industrial bond’s spread over Treasuries widens from 140 bps to 220 bps even though Treasury rates are unchanged.
3 A callable utility bond is redeemed after interest rates fall. The investor must reinvest at lower yields.
4 A high-coupon bond pays coupons as expected, but the investor reinvests those coupons at only 2 percent instead of the 5 percent assumed at purchase.
5 A Canadian investor owns a USD corporate bond and the USD depreciates against CAD.
6 A callable agency bond loses value when expected interest-rate volatility rises.

Answer the following:

  1. Assign the main risk category to each event.
  2. For Events 2 and 3, explain why the bond can lose value even if no contractual default occurs.
  3. Explain which two risks can partly offset each other for a buy-and-hold coupon bond.
  4. Identify one event that illustrates risk from an embedded option.
  5. Explain why understanding the security’s structure is itself a risk-control tool.

Answer.

  1. Event 1 is interest-rate risk. Event 2 is credit spread risk. Event 3 is call risk and reinvestment risk. Event 4 is reinvestment risk. Event 5 is exchange-rate risk. Event 6 is volatility risk for an option-rich security.

  2. In Event 2, the market now requires more compensation for credit or liquidity risk, so the required yield rises even though the issuer has not missed a payment. Price falls because the bond’s promised cash flows are discounted at a higher spread. In Event 3, the investor loses because the issuer exercises a contractual right. The issuer pays the call price, so there is no default, but the investor is forced to give up a high-coupon bond and reinvest at lower rates.

  3. Interest-rate risk and reinvestment risk can partly offset each other. When yields rise, the bond’s market price falls, but future coupons can be reinvested at higher rates. When yields fall, the bond price rises, but coupons are reinvested at lower rates. Immunization strategies are built around balancing these two effects for a target horizon.

  4. Event 3 directly illustrates an embedded call option. Event 6 also involves option exposure because the value of the issuer’s call option increases when volatility rises.

  5. Structure matters because cash-flow timing is not always fixed. A bond may be callable, putable, convertible, floating-rate, inverse-floating, amortizing, inflation-linked, or credit-structured. If the investor treats all bonds like simple fixed-rate bullets, the investor may misjudge price sensitivity, reinvestment exposure, and downside behavior. This is Fabozzi’s “risk risk”: the risk of not understanding the risks being purchased.

1.3 Question 3

An insurance company is comparing a bullet corporate bond with an amortizing asset-backed security. Both securities have final maturities of five years and face values of USD 10,000,000.

Year Bullet bond principal repayment ABS principal repayment
1 0 1,200,000
2 0 1,600,000
3 0 2,100,000
4 0 2,500,000
5 10,000,000 2,600,000

Answer the following:

  1. Compute the weighted average life of the bullet bond.
  2. Compute the weighted average life of the ABS.
  3. Explain why the ABS has less principal-timing exposure than the bullet bond even though final maturity is the same.
  4. Explain one risk that could make the realized life of the ABS differ from the scheduled life.
  5. Discuss why a liability-driven investor might prefer the ABS even if its quoted yield is lower.

Answer.

  1. The bullet bond repays all USD 10,000,000 in year 5. Weighted average life equals 5.00 years:

WAL = (10,000,000 x 5) / 10,000,000 = 5.00.

  1. For the ABS:

WAL = (1,200,000 x 1 + 1,600,000 x 2 + 2,100,000 x 3 + 2,500,000 x 4 + 2,600,000 x 5) / 10,000,000.

The numerator is 1,200,000 + 3,200,000 + 6,300,000 + 10,000,000 + 13,000,000 = 33,700,000. WAL = 33,700,000 / 10,000,000 = 3.37 years.

  1. Final maturity only tells us the last date on which principal is repaid. The ABS returns principal throughout the five-year period, so the investor’s capital is recovered earlier on average. Earlier principal recovery reduces exposure to the issuer and to late-period market conditions, but it also creates reinvestment needs.

  2. Prepayments, defaults, recoveries, or collateral performance can change the realized principal schedule. If borrowers prepay faster, WAL shortens. If collateral defaults or extensions delay principal, WAL can lengthen or cash flows can be impaired.

  3. A liability-driven investor may prefer the ABS if its principal payments match expected liability outflows. Matching cash-flow timing can reduce the need to sell assets later. A lower quoted yield may be acceptable if the ABS reduces reinvestment, liquidity, or asset-liability mismatch risk.

1.4 Question 4

A portfolio manager is comparing four bond sectors for a balanced fixed-income mandate.

Sector Typical issuer/collateral Main compensation required by investors Liquidity comment
Treasury Sovereign government Time value and inflation expectations Very liquid
Municipal revenue bond Local project revenue Project credit, tax status, liquidity Often less liquid
Investment-grade corporate Corporate issuer Credit spread, liquidity, downgrade risk Moderate liquidity
Leveraged loan Senior secured corporate loan Credit risk, liquidity risk, covenant risk Can be weak in stress

Answer the following:

  1. Explain why Treasury yields are often used as benchmark rates.
  2. Explain why the corporate bond should normally trade at a positive spread over Treasuries.
  3. Explain why the municipal bond cannot be compared to a corporate bond only by pretax yield.
  4. Explain why the leveraged loan requires analysis beyond a simple issuer rating.
  5. Recommend two due-diligence questions the manager should ask before buying the leveraged loan.

Answer.

  1. Treasury securities are backed by the sovereign’s taxing authority and are highly liquid. In domestic currency, they are commonly treated as the default-free base rate. Their yields provide a benchmark for the time value of money across maturities.

  2. The corporate bond exposes investors to default risk, downgrade risk, credit spread risk, and lower liquidity than Treasuries. Investors require compensation for those risks, so the corporate yield normally equals the Treasury base rate plus a spread.

  3. Municipal bonds may be tax-exempt. A taxable corporate yield must be converted to an after-tax yield, or the municipal yield must be converted to a taxable-equivalent yield. The investor should compare what is kept after tax, not only the quoted pretax yield.

  4. A leveraged loan depends on borrower leverage, collateral coverage, covenant protection, seniority, loan documentation, liquidity, and refinancing access. A simple rating may not capture recovery risk, covenant leakage, or trading liquidity.

  5. First, ask what collateral supports the loan and how much enterprise value would remain under stress. Second, ask what covenants, liens, baskets, and debt-incurrence permissions are in the credit agreement. These questions target both recovery risk and creditor-protection risk.

1.5 Question 5

A high-net-worth investor wants to buy a convertible bond issued by a technology company. The bond has a 2.25 percent coupon, 7-year maturity, and can be converted into 18 shares of the issuer’s common stock. A straight 7-year bond from the same issuer would require a 6.00 percent coupon.

Answer the following:

  1. Explain why the convertible can be issued with a lower coupon than straight debt.
  2. Identify the embedded option and who owns it.
  3. Explain how the bond behaves if the issuer’s stock price rises sharply.
  4. Explain how it behaves if the stock price falls and credit quality weakens.
  5. Compare this convertible bond with an exchangeable bond.

Answer.

  1. Investors accept a lower coupon because they receive potential equity upside through the conversion feature. Part of the investor’s required return is paid in the form of an embedded option rather than current cash interest.

  2. The embedded option is the right to convert the bond into common shares. The bondholder owns this option. It is valuable when the stock price rises enough that the conversion value exceeds the value of remaining bond cash flows.

  3. If the stock price rises sharply, the convertible begins to behave more like equity. Its price becomes increasingly tied to conversion value, calculated as conversion ratio times stock price. The investor benefits from upside that a straight bondholder would not receive.

  4. If the stock price falls and credit quality weakens, the convertible can lose value from both sides. The equity option becomes less valuable, and the bond floor declines as the issuer’s credit spread widens. The investor still has a debt claim, but that claim is only as strong as the issuer’s ability to pay and the bond’s seniority.

  5. A convertible bond converts into shares of the issuer. An exchangeable bond converts into shares of a company other than the issuer. Exchangeable bonds therefore separate issuer credit risk from the equity risk of the reference shares.

2 Lecture 2: Bond Pricing, Yield Measures, and Total Return

2.1 Question 6

A student analyst prices a 4-year annual-pay bond with face value USD 1,000 and coupon rate 5.50 percent. The market yield is 6.25 percent.

Answer the following:

  1. Write the cash-flow timeline.
  2. Compute the bond price.
  3. State whether the bond trades at a premium, discount, or par.
  4. Explain the economic reason for the result in part c.
  5. If the yield remains at 6.25 percent, explain the expected direction of the bond’s price as maturity approaches.

Answer.

  1. The bond pays coupons of USD 55 in years 1, 2, and 3. In year 4 it pays the final coupon plus principal, or USD 1,055.

  2. Price = 55 / 1.0625 + 55 / 1.0625^2 + 55 / 1.0625^3 + 1,055 / 1.0625^4.

The present values are approximately 51.76, 48.72, 45.85, and 827.76. Total price is approximately USD 974.09.

  1. The bond trades at a discount.

  2. The coupon rate of 5.50 percent is below the market yield of 6.25 percent. Investors require more return than the bond’s coupon provides at par, so the price must fall below par to raise the expected yield to the market-required level.

  3. If the yield remains constant, the price should pull upward toward par as maturity approaches. The discount exists because the coupon is below market, but as fewer below-market coupons remain, the final principal repayment dominates value.

2.2 Question 7

A bond trader is evaluating a semiannual-pay bond with the following terms:

Item Value
Face value USD 1,000
Annual coupon rate 7.20 percent
Years to maturity 6
Yield to maturity 6.40 percent
Coupon frequency Semiannual

Answer the following:

  1. Convert the annual coupon and yield into period values.
  2. Compute the number of remaining coupon periods.
  3. Compute the bond price.
  4. Explain why semiannual compounding changes the calculation.
  5. Explain whether this bond should trade above or below par before doing the arithmetic.

Answer.

  1. The semiannual coupon is 7.20 percent / 2 x USD 1,000 = USD 36. The semiannual yield is 6.40 percent / 2 = 3.20 percent.

  2. Six years with two coupons per year gives 12 remaining periods.

  3. Price = 36 x [1 - (1.032)^(-12)] / 0.032 + 1,000 / 1.032^12. The coupon annuity value is approximately USD 355.37 and the principal present value is approximately USD 685.28. Total price is approximately USD 1,040.65.

  4. Semiannual compounding matters because cash flows occur every six months, not annually. The discount rate per period must match the timing of cash flows. Using a 6.40 percent annual discount rate for six annual periods would discount the wrong cash-flow pattern.

  5. The bond should trade above par because its coupon rate of 7.20 percent is above the yield of 6.40 percent. It pays more coupon income than newly required by the market, so investors bid the price above face value.

2.3 Question 8

A bond is purchased between coupon dates. It has a 5.00 percent annual coupon paid semiannually on USD 1,000 face value. The quoted clean price is 101.40 percent of par. Settlement occurs 72 days after the last coupon, and the coupon period has 180 days.

Answer the following:

  1. Compute the semiannual coupon payment.
  2. Compute accrued interest.
  3. Compute the clean dollar price.
  4. Compute the full or dirty price.
  5. Explain why the buyer pays accrued interest to the seller.

Answer.

  1. The annual coupon is 5.00 percent x USD 1,000 = USD 50. The semiannual coupon is USD 25.

  2. Accrued interest = 25 x 72 / 180 = USD 10.00.

  3. Clean dollar price = 101.40 percent x USD 1,000 = USD 1,014.00.

  4. Full price = clean price + accrued interest = USD 1,014.00 + USD 10.00 = USD 1,024.00.

  5. The seller held the bond for 72 days of the coupon period and economically earned that portion of the next coupon. Because the next coupon will be paid entirely to the buyer, the buyer compensates the seller through accrued interest at settlement.

2.4 Question 9

A client buys a 3-year annual-pay bond for USD 980. The bond has face value USD 1,000 and coupon rate 6.00 percent. The client plans to sell after two years, not hold to maturity. The client expects to reinvest the first coupon at 4.00 percent for one year and expects the bond’s yield at sale to be 5.50 percent.

Answer the following:

  1. Compute the coupon cash flows received during the two-year horizon.
  2. Compute the future value at year 2 of the first coupon after reinvestment.
  3. Compute the expected sale price at year 2.
  4. Compute total future dollars at year 2.
  5. Compute the two-year horizon return.

Answer.

  1. The bond pays USD 60 at the end of year 1 and USD 60 at the end of year 2.

  2. The year-1 coupon is reinvested for one year at 4.00 percent, so its year-2 value is 60 x 1.04 = USD 62.40.

  3. At year 2, the bond has one year remaining and will pay USD 1,060 at year 3. If the sale yield is 5.50 percent, sale price = 1,060 / 1.055 = USD 1,004.74.

  4. Total future dollars at year 2 = reinvested first coupon + second coupon + sale price = 62.40 + 60.00 + 1,004.74 = USD 1,127.14.

  5. The two-year horizon return solves 980 x (1 + R)^2 = 1,127.14. Therefore R = (1,127.14 / 980)^(1/2) - 1 = 7.25 percent approximately. This return differs from the original yield because it depends on the reinvestment rate and sale yield.

2.5 Question 10

A firm has issued a floating-rate note with quarterly coupons. The coupon rate each quarter is 3-month SOFR plus a quoted margin of 1.25 percent. The note has face value USD 5,000,000. There is a coupon floor of 2.00 percent and a cap of 6.50 percent. The next three SOFR reset rates are assumed to be:

Quarter SOFR at reset
1 0.50 percent
2 3.80 percent
3 6.10 percent

Answer the following:

  1. Compute the uncapped annual coupon rate for each quarter.
  2. Apply the floor and cap.
  3. Compute each quarterly coupon payment.
  4. Explain how the cap changes investor exposure.
  5. Explain why discount margin is more natural than yield-to-maturity for this security.

Answer.

  1. The uncapped annual coupon rates are SOFR + 1.25 percent. Quarter 1: 1.75 percent. Quarter 2: 5.05 percent. Quarter 3: 7.35 percent.

  2. The floor is 2.00 percent and the cap is 6.50 percent. Quarter 1 is floored at 2.00 percent. Quarter 2 remains 5.05 percent. Quarter 3 is capped at 6.50 percent.

  3. Quarterly coupon = annual coupon rate / 4 x face value. Quarter 1 = 2.00 percent / 4 x 5,000,000 = USD 25,000. Quarter 2 = 5.05 percent / 4 x 5,000,000 = USD 63,125. Quarter 3 = 6.50 percent / 4 x 5,000,000 = USD 81,250.

  4. The cap limits the investor’s benefit when short rates rise sharply. Without the cap, Quarter 3 would pay 7.35 percent annualized. With the cap, the investor receives only 6.50 percent. The issuer has effectively retained protection against very high reference rates.

  5. Yield-to-maturity assumes a fixed set of cash flows. The floater’s future coupons depend on future SOFR resets and cap/floor behavior. Discount margin frames valuation as a spread over expected reference rates, which better matches the instrument’s economics.

3 Lecture 3: Duration, Convexity, and Portfolio Strategies

3.1 Question 11

A risk manager studies two annual-pay option-free bonds, each with face value USD 1,000 and yield of 5.00 percent.

Bond Coupon rate Maturity
X 2.00 percent 10 years
Y 8.00 percent 10 years

Answer the following:

  1. Before calculating, predict which bond has greater duration.
  2. Explain the cash-flow timing intuition behind your prediction.
  3. Compute the approximate price of each bond.
  4. Explain why the lower-coupon bond is more volatile.
  5. State one case where this ranking could fail.

Answer.

  1. Bond X should have greater duration.

  2. Bond X pays small coupons, so more of its value comes from the principal payment at year 10. Bond Y pays larger coupons, which return more value earlier. Duration is based on present-value weighted timing, so the bond with later-weighted cash flows has higher duration.

  3. Bond X price = 20 x [1 - 1.05^(-10)] / 0.05 + 1,000 / 1.05^10 = approximately USD 768.35. Bond Y price = 80 x [1 - 1.05^(-10)] / 0.05 + 1,000 / 1.05^10 = approximately USD 1,231.65.

  4. A small change in yield has a larger effect when more value is concentrated in distant cash flows. Distant cash flows are discounted for more periods, so their present values are more sensitive to the discount rate.

  5. The ranking could fail if the bonds have embedded options or materially different credit behavior. For example, a callable high-coupon bond can have state-dependent effective duration, and an inverse floater can have unusually high duration despite a short contractual maturity.

3.2 Question 12

A portfolio contains three bonds. The manager wants to estimate portfolio duration.

Bond Market value Modified duration
Treasury note USD 4,000,000 3.8
Corporate bond USD 3,500,000 6.2
Callable agency note USD 2,500,000 4.4

Answer the following:

  1. Compute each bond’s market-value weight.
  2. Compute portfolio modified duration.
  3. Estimate the portfolio dollar price change for a 35 bp parallel increase in yields.
  4. Explain one limitation of this estimate for the callable agency note.
  5. Explain how key rate duration could improve the analysis.

Answer.

  1. Total market value is USD 10,000,000. The weights are 40.0 percent Treasury, 35.0 percent corporate, and 25.0 percent callable agency note.

  2. Portfolio duration = 0.40 x 3.8 + 0.35 x 6.2 + 0.25 x 4.4 = 1.52 + 2.17 + 1.10 = 4.79.

  3. Approximate percentage change = -4.79 x 0.0035 = -1.6765 percent. Dollar change = -1.6765 percent x 10,000,000 = approximately -USD 167,650.

  4. The callable agency note has option risk. Its effective duration can change when rates and volatility change because the probability of being called is state-dependent. A static duration estimate can miss that option-driven behavior.

  5. Key rate duration separates exposure by maturity point on the curve. If the portfolio is most sensitive to the 5-year point and the curve twist occurs there, total duration may miss the risk. Key rate duration helps reveal exposure to nonparallel yield-curve changes.

3.3 Question 13

A bond’s current price is 104.60. If yield decreases by 50 bps, the model price is 108.90. If yield increases by 50 bps, the model price is 100.55.

Answer the following:

  1. Estimate empirical modified duration.
  2. Estimate empirical convexity.
  3. Use duration alone to approximate the price change for a 75 bp yield increase.
  4. Use duration plus convexity for the same 75 bp increase.
  5. Explain why the two approximations differ.

Answer.

  1. Empirical modified duration = (P_minus - P_plus) / (2 x P0 x shock). The shock is 0.005. Duration = (108.90 - 100.55) / (2 x 104.60 x 0.005) = 8.35 / 1.046 = 7.98.

  2. Empirical convexity = (P_minus + P_plus - 2P0) / (P0 x shock^2). Convexity = (108.90 + 100.55 - 2 x 104.60) / (104.60 x 0.005^2) = 0.25 / 0.002615 = 95.60.

  3. Duration-only percentage change = -7.98 x 0.0075 = -5.99 percent. Dollar price change = -5.99 percent x 104.60 = about -6.26 price points.

  4. Duration-plus-convexity percentage change = -7.98 x 0.0075 + 0.5 x 95.60 x 0.0075^2. The convexity term is about 0.269 percent, so the total is about -5.72 percent. Dollar change = -5.72 percent x 104.60 = about -5.98 price points.

  5. Duration is a linear tangent approximation. Convexity recognizes curvature. For an option-free bond with positive convexity, the convexity term reduces the estimated loss when yields rise and increases the estimated gain when yields fall.

3.4 Question 14

A benchmark-aware manager has the same total duration as the benchmark but different key-rate exposures.

Portfolio 2-year KRD 5-year KRD 10-year KRD 30-year KRD Total
Benchmark 0.8 1.7 2.0 1.0 5.5
Manager 0.2 2.8 1.1 1.4 5.5

The manager expects a curve steepening where 2-year yields fall 30 bps, 5-year yields fall 10 bps, 10-year yields rise 15 bps, and 30-year yields rise 35 bps.

Answer the following:

  1. Compute the approximate percentage return impact for the benchmark.
  2. Compute the approximate percentage return impact for the manager.
  3. Determine which performs better under this scenario.
  4. Explain why equal total duration was not enough.
  5. Identify what active view the manager has implicitly expressed.

Answer.

  1. Approximate return impact is the negative sum of KRD times rate change. Use decimal yield changes. Benchmark impact = -[0.8(-0.0030) + 1.7(-0.0010) + 2.0(0.0015) + 1.0(0.0035)]. This equals -[-0.0024 - 0.0017 + 0.0030 + 0.0035] = -0.0024, or -0.24 percent.

  2. Manager impact = -[0.2(-0.0030) + 2.8(-0.0010) + 1.1(0.0015) + 1.4(0.0035)]. This equals -[-0.0006 - 0.0028 + 0.00165 + 0.0049] = -0.00315, or -0.315 percent.

  3. The benchmark performs better by about 0.075 percent, or 7.5 bps.

  4. Equal total duration only means equal exposure to a parallel shift. The actual scenario is nonparallel. The manager is overweight 5-year and 30-year exposure and underweight 2-year and 10-year exposure relative to the benchmark, so performance depends on where the curve moves.

  5. The manager is positioned for the 5-year sector to perform well relative to the long end, or for less damage from 30-year rates than actually occurred. The portfolio is not neutral to curve reshaping.

3.5 Question 15

A pension plan has liabilities with present value USD 100 million and modified duration 9.0. The asset portfolio has present value USD 100 million and modified duration 6.5. The CIO considers adding Treasury futures to increase duration.

Answer the following:

  1. Explain the duration gap.
  2. Estimate asset value change and liability value change if yields fall by 50 bps.
  3. Explain why the plan’s funded status changes.
  4. Compute the additional dollar duration needed to match liability duration.
  5. Explain why leverage through futures introduces risk beyond duration matching.

Answer.

  1. The assets and liabilities have equal present value, but liabilities are more rate-sensitive. Liability duration is 9.0 while asset duration is 6.5. The plan is under-duration: falling rates increase liabilities more than assets.

  2. Asset percentage change = -6.5 x (-0.0050) = +3.25 percent, or +USD 3.25 million. Liability percentage change = -9.0 x (-0.0050) = +4.50 percent, or +USD 4.50 million.

  3. Funded status worsens because liabilities rise by USD 4.50 million while assets rise by only USD 3.25 million. The plan loses USD 1.25 million of surplus or becomes more underfunded.

  4. Dollar duration needed = (liability duration - asset duration) x asset value = (9.0 - 6.5) x 100,000,000 = USD 250,000,000 duration-dollars per 100 percent yield change. Equivalently, the plan needs USD 25,000 of DV01 because 250,000,000 x 0.0001 = 25,000.

  5. Futures can efficiently add duration, but they introduce margin, basis, roll, liquidity, and operational risks. The futures contract may not perfectly match the liability curve exposure. A hedge can reduce parallel-rate risk while leaving curve, inflation, and collateral risks.

4 Lecture 4: Benchmark Spreads and Term Structure

4.1 Question 16

A credit analyst observes the following bonds, each with 7-year maturity:

Bond Issuer type Yield 7-year Treasury yield
A Federal agency 4.42 percent 4.10 percent
B A-rated utility 5.05 percent 4.10 percent
C BB industrial 7.35 percent 4.10 percent

Answer the following:

  1. Compute each benchmark spread in basis points.
  2. Explain why spreads differ across issuer types.
  3. Identify which spread may include the largest credit-risk premium.
  4. Identify one non-credit reason spreads may differ.
  5. Explain why spread is not the same as expected default loss.

Answer.

  1. Spread = bond yield - Treasury yield. Bond A spread = 0.32 percent = 32 bps. Bond B spread = 0.95 percent = 95 bps. Bond C spread = 3.25 percent = 325 bps.

  2. Different issuers have different default risk, downgrade risk, liquidity, legal protections, and investor bases. Agency debt is close to Treasury quality but less liquid or less explicitly guaranteed depending on issuer. The utility has corporate credit risk. The BB industrial has speculative-grade credit risk and therefore requires much more compensation.

  3. Bond C likely includes the largest credit-risk premium because it is below investment grade and has the widest spread.

  4. Liquidity is a major non-credit reason. A smaller or less frequently traded issue may need a higher yield even if default risk is similar. Tax status, financing eligibility, and embedded options can also affect spreads.

  5. Spread includes expected default loss, risk premium for uncertainty around default, downgrade risk, liquidity premium, tax effects, and sometimes option compensation. Expected default loss is only one component.

4.2 Question 17

You are given annual-pay Treasury securities with face value 100.

Maturity Coupon rate Price
1 year 0.00 percent 96.6184
2 years 4.00 percent 99.1375
3 years 5.00 percent 100.1150

Answer the following:

  1. Bootstrap the 1-year spot rate.
  2. Bootstrap the 2-year spot rate.
  3. Bootstrap the 3-year spot rate.
  4. Explain why coupon-bond yields are not enough to construct the spot curve directly.
  5. Explain how bootstrapping avoids double counting.

Answer.

  1. The 1-year zero price gives z1 = 100 / 96.6184 - 1 = 3.50 percent.

  2. For the 2-year bond: 99.1375 = 4 / 1.035 + 104 / (1 + z2)^2. The first coupon PV is 3.8647. The remaining value assigned to year 2 is 95.2728. Thus (1 + z2)^2 = 104 / 95.2728 = 1.09161, so z2 = 4.48 percent approximately.

  3. For the 3-year bond: 100.1150 = 5 / 1.035 + 5 / (1.0448)^2 + 105 / (1 + z3)^3. The first two coupon present values are about 4.8309 and 4.5804. The remaining value is 90.7037. Thus (1 + z3)^3 = 105 / 90.7037, so z3 = 5.00 percent approximately.

  4. A coupon-bond yield is a single internal rate applied to all cash flows. A spot curve needs a separate discount rate for each maturity cash flow. Coupon yields mix information from multiple cash-flow dates.

  5. Bootstrapping values the earliest cash flows using already solved spot rates. It then solves only for the next unknown spot rate from the remaining price. That prevents the analyst from treating the full coupon bond yield as if it applied directly to every cash-flow date.

4.3 Question 18

A 3-year annual-pay corporate bond has face value 100, coupon rate 6.00 percent, and market price 101.20. Treasury spot rates are:

Year Treasury spot rate
1 3.40 percent
2 4.00 percent
3 4.60 percent

Answer the following:

  1. Compute the Treasury-curve value using spot rates and no credit spread.
  2. Explain whether the corporate bond is rich or cheap relative to Treasury discounting alone.
  3. Find an approximate constant spread over the spot curve by trial using 60 bps and 90 bps.
  4. Explain why this constant spread is conceptually better than comparing only to the 3-year Treasury yield.
  5. Name one limitation of a constant spread.

Answer.

  1. Treasury-curve value = 6 / 1.034 + 6 / 1.04^2 + 106 / 1.046^3. This is approximately 5.80 + 5.55 + 92.71 = 104.06.

  2. The corporate bond trades at 101.20, below the Treasury-discounted value of 104.06. That is expected because investors require extra spread for corporate credit and liquidity risk.

  3. With a 60 bp spread, discount rates are 4.00 percent, 4.60 percent, and 5.20 percent. Price is approximately 102.41. With a 90 bp spread, discount rates are 4.30 percent, 4.90 percent, and 5.50 percent. Price is approximately 101.59. The market price of 101.20 implies a spread slightly above 90 bps, roughly around 104 bps by interpolation.

  4. The constant spread discounts each cash flow at the matching Treasury spot rate plus spread. Comparing only to the 3-year Treasury yield ignores the coupon payments in years 1 and 2.

4.4 Question 19

The annual spot curve is:

Maturity Spot rate
1 year 3.20 percent
2 years 3.80 percent
3 years 4.30 percent
5 years 4.90 percent

Answer the following:

  1. Compute the 1-year forward rate beginning one year from now.
  2. Compute the 1-year forward rate beginning two years from now.
  3. Compute the 2-year forward rate beginning three years from now.
  4. Explain the break-even interpretation of the forward rate.
  5. Explain why the forward curve may differ from the market’s actual expected future short rates.

Answer.

  1. 1 + f1,2 = 1.038^2 / 1.032. Therefore f1,2 = 4.40 percent approximately.

  2. 1 + f2,3 = 1.043^3 / 1.038^2. Therefore f2,3 = 5.31 percent approximately.

  3. The 2-year forward rate from year 3 to year 5 satisfies (1 + f3,5)^2 = 1.049^5 / 1.043^3. Therefore f3,5 is approximately 5.81 percent.

4.5 Question 20

A curve strategist observes the following Treasury yields:

Maturity Current yield
2 years 4.90 percent
5 years 4.45 percent
10 years 4.15 percent
30 years 4.05 percent

The strategist believes the central bank will cut short rates aggressively while long-term inflation uncertainty will keep 30-year yields stable or slightly higher.

Answer the following:

  1. Classify the current curve shape.
  2. Describe the expected curve movement.
  3. Propose a duration-neutral curve trade that benefits from the view.
  4. Explain the main risk if the view is wrong.
  5. Explain why total duration neutrality does not eliminate curve risk.

Answer.

  1. The curve is inverted because shorter maturities yield more than longer maturities.

  2. The expected movement is bull steepening: short yields fall substantially while long yields fall less, remain stable, or rise slightly. The curve becomes steeper because the short end declines relative to the long end.

  3. A duration-neutral trade could overweight 2-year Treasuries and underweight 30-year Treasuries, sizing the positions so total dollar duration is approximately neutral. The position profits if 2-year yields fall more than 30-year yields.

  4. The main risk is that the curve does not steepen. If long yields fall more than short yields, the short-2-year/long-30-year relative view loses. If all yields rise, imperfect duration neutrality and convexity differences can also hurt.

  5. Total duration neutrality hedges only a parallel shift. It does not hedge changes in curve shape. A trade can have zero total duration but large positive exposure to the 2-year key rate and large negative exposure to the 30-year key rate.

5.1 Question 21

A money-market portfolio buys a 182-day Treasury bill with face value USD 5,000,000 and bank discount yield of 4.80 percent.

Answer the following:

  1. Compute the purchase price using the bank discount formula.
  2. Compute the dollar discount.
  3. Compute the bond-equivalent yield using a 365-day year.
  4. Explain why the bond-equivalent yield is higher than the bank discount yield.
  5. Explain why T-bills are still not riskless for every investor even though they are default-free.

Answer.

  1. Price = Face x [1 - discount yield x days / 360]. Price = 5,000,000 x [1 - 0.048 x 182 / 360] = USD 4,878,666.67.

  2. Dollar discount = 5,000,000 - 4,878,666.67 = USD 121,333.33.

  3. BEY = (Face - Price) / Price x 365 / days. BEY = 121,333.33 / 4,878,666.67 x 365 / 182 = 4.99 percent approximately.

  4. Bank discount yield divides the discount by face value and annualizes on a 360-day basis. BEY divides by the actual investment price and annualizes on a 365-day basis. Because price is below face value, using price as the denominator raises the yield.

  5. T-bills have negligible default risk, but investors can still face reinvestment risk, inflation risk, opportunity cost, and liquidity needs. A foreign investor also faces exchange-rate risk.

5.3 Question 23

A corporate issuer has the following capital structure before entering Chapter 11:

Claim Amount outstanding Seniority/collateral
Revolving credit facility USD 200 million Senior secured
First-lien term loan USD 600 million Senior secured
Senior unsecured bonds USD 700 million Senior unsecured
Subordinated notes USD 300 million Subordinated
Preferred equity USD 100 million Equity-like

Estimated enterprise value in reorganization is USD 1.25 billion.

Answer the following:

  1. Allocate recovery by seniority using a strict priority waterfall.
  2. Compute recovery percentage for each debt class.
  3. Explain why actual Chapter 11 outcomes can differ from strict priority.
  4. Explain why secured debt usually has lower required spreads than subordinated debt.
  5. Identify one covenant that could have protected unsecured bondholders before distress.

Answer.

  1. The USD 1.25 billion enterprise value first pays senior secured claims. The revolver receives USD 200 million and the first-lien term loan receives USD 600 million, leaving USD 450 million. The senior unsecured bonds receive that USD 450 million against USD 700 million of claims. Nothing remains for subordinated notes or preferred equity.

  2. Revolver recovery = 100 percent. First-lien term loan recovery = 100 percent. Senior unsecured bond recovery = 450 / 700 = 64.3 percent. Subordinated notes recovery = 0 percent. Preferred equity recovery = 0 percent.

  3. Actual reorganizations involve negotiation, litigation risk, time value, debtor-in-possession financing, valuation disputes, and the need to gain creditor approval. Some junior claimants may receive value to avoid delay or because valuation is contested.

  4. Secured debt has collateral and senior priority, so expected recovery is usually higher if default occurs. Lower expected loss supports a lower spread. Subordinated debt absorbs losses after senior claims and therefore requires higher compensation.

  5. A limitation on additional secured debt could have protected unsecured bondholders. Without that covenant, the issuer might issue more secured debt ahead of unsecured bonds, reducing their recovery in default.

5.4 Question 24

A firm is choosing between commercial paper and a 5-year medium-term note to finance operations.

Instrument Maturity Rate type Intended use Investor concern
Commercial paper 90 days Discount Working capital Rollover and liquidity support
Medium-term note 5 years Fixed coupon Long-term funding Credit spread and covenants

Answer the following:

  1. Explain why commercial paper is usually issued at a discount.
  2. Explain why backup bank lines matter for commercial paper issuers.
  3. Explain why an MTN program gives the issuer flexibility.
  4. Explain which instrument creates more refinancing risk.
  5. Explain why the 5-year MTN likely requires a higher yield than 90-day commercial paper for the same issuer.

Answer.

  1. Commercial paper is a short-term zero-coupon or discount instrument. Investors buy it below face value and receive face value at maturity. The discount is the interest earned.

  2. Backup bank lines reassure investors that the issuer can repay maturing paper if the commercial paper market temporarily closes. Without liquidity support, a solvent issuer can still face a rollover crisis.

  3. An MTN program lets the issuer sell notes continuously or episodically with different maturities, coupons, currencies, and structures. The issuer can tailor debt issuance to investor demand and funding needs.

  4. Commercial paper creates more refinancing risk because it matures in 90 days and must be rolled frequently. The MTN locks in funding for five years.

  5. The MTN exposes investors to five years of credit deterioration, interest-rate risk, liquidity risk, and downgrade risk. The 90-day paper has much shorter exposure if it is repaid on time. Investors normally require a higher yield for the longer commitment.

5.5 Question 25

A credit portfolio includes a leveraged loan and a high-yield bond from the same issuer.

Instrument Coupon Seniority Security Rate type Market price
Leveraged loan SOFR + 375 bps Senior Secured Floating 98.50
High-yield bond 7.75 percent Senior subordinated Unsecured Fixed 94.00

Answer the following:

  1. Compare their interest-rate sensitivity.
  2. Compare expected recovery in default.
  3. Explain why the high-yield bond may offer a higher coupon.
  4. Explain one risk in the leveraged loan that is not solved by floating-rate coupons.
  5. Explain how a CLO can transform a pool of leveraged loans into securities with different risks.

Answer.

  1. The leveraged loan has lower interest-rate duration because its coupon resets with SOFR. The high-yield bond has fixed coupons and therefore more price sensitivity to changes in market yields.

  2. The leveraged loan is senior secured, so it usually has higher expected recovery. The high-yield bond is subordinated and unsecured, so it stands behind senior secured claims and likely recovers less in default.

  3. The high-yield bond offers a higher coupon because investors need compensation for fixed-rate duration, lower recovery, subordination, weaker collateral protection, and credit risk.

  4. Floating coupons do not eliminate credit risk. If the issuer’s business weakens, loan price can fall due to wider spreads or higher default probability. The loan can also have liquidity risk, covenant risk, and refinancing risk.

  5. A CLO buys a diversified pool of leveraged loans and issues tranches. Senior tranches have first claim on cash flows and lower risk, while junior and equity tranches absorb losses first and have higher risk. The structure reallocates credit risk rather than eliminating it.

A manager compares two candidate portfolios against the same benchmark.

Portfolio Expected active return Predicted tracking error

  4. Explain why Portfolio B may still be chosen by an active manager.
  5. Explain why a passive mandate would reject both portfolios if the tracking error budget is 25 bps.

Answer.

A factor model reports the following active exposures and monthly factor volatilities.

6.5 Question 30

A portfolio manager rebalances a USD 100 million bond portfolio. Before rebalancing, predicted tracking error is 14.8 bps per month, duration gap is 0.32 years, and bank-sector active weight is 8.0 percent. The client limits are 12.0 bps tracking error, 0.25 years duration gap, and 5.0 percent bank active weight.   1. Identify which constraints are violated before rebalancing.
  2. Identify whether the proposed trades bring the portfolio inside the constraints.

  4. Explain why the manager should compare the risk reduction with transaction cost.
  5. Explain why rebalancing should not automatically eliminate every active view.
