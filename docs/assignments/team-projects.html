Fixed-Income Team Projects

BUS 411

Common Scope and Standards

Each project is designed for a team of two or three students. The final product is a 10–12 minute team presentation supported by a concise analyst-style exhibit package. All team members should participate in the analysis and presentation.

  • Financial reasoning: Emphasize interpretation, assumptions, tradeoffs, and recommendations. Coding is optional and should remain limited to simple data retrieval, calculations, or charts. A spreadsheet is sufficient for every project.
  • Reproducibility: State the observation date, units, conventions, formulas, and transformations. Submit the source data or a clean extract, plus the spreadsheet or short script used to produce the exhibits.
  • Attribution: Cite every external dataset, market quote, filing, figure, and factual claim. Distinguish observed data from assumptions and estimates.
  • Scope discipline: Use a manageable sample and explain exclusions. When live or historical access is difficult, teams may use an instructor-provided dated snapshot of the named source.
  • Professional judgment: Identify limitations and discuss how they affect the conclusion. These projects are educational analyses, not investment advice.
  • Presentation standard: Use legible figures, label axes and units, define fixed-income terminology, and end with a clear conclusion. Appendices may contain supporting calculations but are not additional presentation time.

Unless a project says otherwise, submit: (1) presentation slides in PDF, (2) a reproducible exhibit package, and (3) a one-page source and assumptions note.

1 Lecture 1: Bond Features, Structures, Markets, and Risks

1.1 Project 1: Read the Bond Before Recommending It

Scenario. A wealth-management team is choosing between two recently issued bonds from the same or comparable issuers. Your team must explain how contractual features change the investor’s cash flows and risks.

Objectives

  • Extract and compare coupon, maturity, seniority, security, call, sinking-fund, and covenant terms.
  • Map each feature to interest-rate, credit, liquidity, reinvestment, inflation, and optionality risk.
  • Recommend which bond better fits a stated conservative-income mandate.

Data sources. Issuer prospectus supplements and annual reports filed on SEC EDGAR or SEDAR+; FINRA Fixed Income Data/Bond Center for U.S. corporate bond reference information and recent trades; an instructor-provided prospectus snapshot if needed.

Deliverables. A one-page term sheet comparison; a risk map with at least six feature-to-risk links; a mandate-based recommendation with two limitations; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Contract-term accuracy, 25; feature-to-cash-flow analysis, 25; risk reasoning, 25; recommendation and limitations, 15; presentation clarity and source attribution, 10.

1.2 Project 2: Funding Choice for a Corporate Treasurer

Scenario. A corporate treasurer must raise medium-term funding and is considering a fixed-rate bond, floating-rate note, or bank loan.

Objectives

  • Compare cash-flow uncertainty, refinancing risk, covenants, seniority, and investor base across the three structures.
  • Relate the choice to the firm’s asset duration and rate outlook without relying on a directional forecast alone.
  • Propose a defensible funding structure and identify the conditions under which it should change.

Data sources. A public issuer’s latest annual report, debt footnote, and investor presentation; benchmark rates from FRED or the Bank of Canada; sample loan or note terms from issuer filings; instructor-provided terms where loan details are private.

Deliverables. A funding-needs diagnosis; a three-column structure comparison; one simple annual interest-cost scenario table; a recommendation and trigger conditions; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Issuer and funding-needs analysis, 20; structure comparison, 30; scenario design and calculations, 20; recommendation and contingencies, 20; presentation and documentation, 10.

2 Lecture 2: Bond Pricing, Yield Measures, and Total Return

2.1 Project 3: Price, Yield, and Horizon Return Case

Scenario. An investor may buy a coupon bond but expects to sell it before maturity. Evaluate whether yield to maturity is an adequate decision measure.

Objectives

  • Reproduce the quoted bond price from promised cash flows and yield conventions.
  • Calculate current yield, yield to maturity, and a two- or three-year horizon return.
  • Show how reinvestment rates and the sale yield affect realized return.

Data sources. U.S. Treasury or Bank of Canada bond data for an option-free government bond; FINRA Fixed Income Data/TRACE for a corporate alternative; official security terms from TreasuryDirect, the Bank of Canada, or an issuer filing.

Deliverables. A cash-flow timeline; transparent price and yield calculations; a three-scenario horizon-return table; an explanation of why realized return differs from YTM; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Cash-flow and convention accuracy, 20; price and yield calculations, 25; horizon-return scenarios, 30; interpretation, 15; presentation and reproducibility, 10.

2.2 Project 4: Premium, Par, or Discount?

Scenario. A client believes that a discount bond is automatically a better bargain than a premium bond. Test the claim using matched securities.

Objectives

  • Select two government or high-quality corporate bonds with similar maturity and credit exposure but different coupons.
  • Explain the relationship among coupon rate, market yield, price, pull to par, and accrued interest.
  • Compare after-tax or accounting considerations qualitatively where relevant.

Data sources. U.S. Treasury marketable-security data or Bank of Canada benchmark bond data; FINRA Fixed Income Data for corporate examples; official tax-agency guidance or accounting-policy disclosures for qualitative context.

Deliverables. A matched-bond table; clean- and dirty-price reconciliation for one settlement date; a price path or pull-to-par exhibit under a constant-yield assumption; a client-facing conclusion; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Security matching and data quality, 20; pricing and accrued-interest work, 30; pull-to-par analysis, 20; financial explanation and caveats, 20; presentation quality, 10.

3 Lecture 3: Duration, Convexity, and Portfolio Strategies

3.1 Project 5: Immunize a Known Liability

Scenario. A small institution must fund a known payment in five to eight years using two or three government bonds.

Objectives

  • Match present value and duration between the asset portfolio and the liability.
  • Explain why duration matching provides only local protection.
  • Stress-test the position under parallel and simple nonparallel yield-curve changes.

Data sources. U.S. Treasury Daily Treasury Par Yield Curve Rates, TreasuryDirect, or Bank of Canada benchmark yields and bond terms; an instructor-provided set of bond prices and cash flows if necessary.

Deliverables. Liability assumptions; bond selection and weights; present-value, duration, and convexity calculations; at least three rate-shock results; a rebalancing note; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Liability framing, 15; immunization calculations, 30; shock analysis, 25; interpretation and rebalancing judgment, 20; presentation and auditability, 10.

3.2 Project 6: When Convexity Earns Its Keep

Scenario. Two bonds have similar duration but different convexity. A portfolio manager asks when paying for greater convexity is worthwhile.

Objectives

  • Construct or identify a duration-matched bond pair or small portfolios.
  • Compare duration-only and duration-plus-convexity price estimates with full repricing.
  • Explain the role of shock size, direction, yield volatility, and carry.

Data sources. Government bond terms and yields from the U.S. Treasury or Bank of Canada; prices from official sources or FINRA Fixed Income Data; instructor-provided option-free bond snapshots are acceptable.

Deliverables. A duration-matched comparison; repricing results for at least five yield shocks; an approximation-error chart; a discussion of convexity benefit versus likely cost; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Matching design, 20; duration and convexity calculations, 25; full-repricing comparison, 25; economic interpretation, 20; presentation and source note, 10.

4 Lecture 4: Benchmark Spreads and Term Structure

4.1 Project 7: Build and Read a Government Yield Curve

Scenario. Prepare a morning curve briefing for a fixed-income desk on two dates with meaningfully different market conditions.

Objectives

  • Construct comparable government yield curves and calculate key slopes.
  • Separate changes in level, slope, and curvature.
  • Link curve movement cautiously to contemporaneous policy and macroeconomic information.

Data sources. FRED Treasury constant-maturity series, U.S. Treasury Daily Treasury Par Yield Curve Rates, or Bank of Canada selected bond yields; policy releases from the Federal Reserve or Bank of Canada.

Deliverables. Two dated curve plots; 2s10s, 5s30s, and one curvature measure; a change decomposition; a short event timeline; a desk briefing conclusion; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Data consistency and curve construction, 25; slope and curvature analysis, 25; event interpretation, 20; risk implications, 20; presentation and attribution, 10.

4.2 Project 8: Bootstrap a Discount Curve by Hand

Scenario. A valuation team needs discount factors and spot rates from a small set of coupon-bearing government securities.

Objectives

  • Explain the sequential logic of bootstrapping.
  • Derive discount factors and spot rates using four to six instruments.
  • Validate the curve by repricing the input instruments and discuss interpolation risk.

Data sources. An instructor-provided internally consistent Treasury or Government of Canada price snapshot; alternatively, official bill and bond terms from the U.S. Treasury or Bank of Canada with same-date prices from a documented source.

Deliverables. A cash-flow matrix; step-by-step bootstrap calculations; discount-factor and spot-rate curves; repricing errors; an interpolation and convention note; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Instrument and convention setup, 20; bootstrap accuracy, 35; repricing validation, 20; interpolation and limitation analysis, 15; presentation and reproducibility, 10.

5 Lecture 5: Treasury and Corporate Debt Markets

5.1 Project 9: On-the-Run Liquidity and Treasury Specialness

Scenario. A relative-value analyst observes that two similar Treasury securities trade at different yields and must explain why.

Objectives

  • Compare an on-the-run security with a nearby off-the-run security.
  • Distinguish duration and cash-flow differences from liquidity, benchmark demand, and repo specialness.
  • Assess whether the yield difference is compensation for credit risk.

Data sources. U.S. Treasury auction results and security details; Treasury yield and price data from official releases or FRED; Federal Reserve Bank of New York repo data and publications; an instructor-provided same-date quote snapshot.

Deliverables. A matched-security profile; yield and duration comparison; a qualitative decomposition of the spread; a repo-market evidence exhibit; a relative-value conclusion with limitations; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Security matching, 20; yield and risk comparison, 25; liquidity and repo reasoning, 30; conclusion and limitations, 15; presentation and sourcing, 10.

5.2 Project 10: Credit Event Anatomy

Scenario. A corporate issuer experiences a downgrade, earnings shock, acquisition, or refinancing event. Explain how bondholders were affected.

Objectives

  • Build a dated event timeline using public information.
  • Compare bond spread or price behavior before and after the event.
  • Separate Treasury-rate effects from issuer-specific spread effects and discuss expected recovery or covenant implications.

Data sources. FINRA TRACE transaction data or FINRA bond market information; issuer filings and releases from SEC EDGAR or SEDAR+; rating-agency public releases; matched Treasury yields from FRED.

Deliverables. An event timeline; bond and benchmark exhibit; approximate rate-versus-spread price attribution; covenant, seniority, or recovery discussion; a bondholder conclusion; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Event research and chronology, 20; market-data analysis, 25; rate/spread attribution, 25; credit reasoning, 20; presentation and citations, 10.

6 Lecture 6: Bond Portfolio Construction

6.1 Project 11: Build a Practical Bond Index Sample

Scenario. A small fund cannot hold every security in a broad bond index and needs a transparent sampling portfolio.

Objectives

  • Design cells based on maturity, sector, quality, and duration.
  • Select a feasible sample that resembles the benchmark’s major exposures.
  • Evaluate tracking-error sources, liquidity, turnover, and transaction-cost tradeoffs.

Data sources. Public fact sheets and holdings for a broad bond ETF or index fund; issuer and security data from fund disclosures and FINRA Fixed Income Data; instructor-provided benchmark weights where full index constituents are unavailable.

Deliverables. A benchmark exposure table; cell definitions; a 10- to 20-bond proposed sample or stylized sample; exposure gaps; a qualitative tracking-error budget; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Benchmark diagnosis, 20; cell design, 25; portfolio selection and exposure matching, 25; implementation tradeoffs, 20; presentation and documentation, 10.

6.2 Project 12: Rebalance Under Real-World Constraints

Scenario. A bond portfolio has drifted outside duration, sector, issuer, and liquidity limits. The manager can make no more than five trades.

Objectives

  • Diagnose active exposures relative to a benchmark or policy portfolio.
  • Choose trades that reduce unintended risk while preserving an intentional view.
  • Account for lot sizes, bid-ask costs, liquidity, and turnover.

Data sources. Public bond ETF holdings and fact sheets; FINRA Fixed Income Data/TRACE for trading activity; issuer terms from filings; an instructor-provided portfolio, benchmark, and bid-ask snapshot.

Deliverables. Before-trade exposure dashboard; proposed trades and rationale; after-trade exposures; estimated transaction cost; rejected alternatives; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Exposure diagnosis, 25; trade design, 25; constraint and cost treatment, 25; before/after decision quality, 15; presentation and reproducibility, 10.

7 Lecture 7: Interest-Rate Models

7.1 Project 13: Vasicek as a Risk Scenario Generator

Scenario. A risk committee wants to know what a simple mean-reverting short-rate model can and cannot say about future rates.

Objectives

  • Estimate or choose defensible Vasicek parameters from a historical short-rate series.
  • Explain mean reversion, long-run mean, volatility, and the possibility of negative rates.
  • Compare model-implied one-year rate distributions with historical changes.

Data sources. Effective federal funds rate or Treasury bill rates from FRED; overnight or Treasury bill rates from the Bank of Canada; an instructor-provided clean monthly series and parameter template.

Deliverables. Parameter rationale; a spreadsheet simulation or instructor-provided simulation output; a distribution and three sample paths; historical comparison; a model-use and model-risk memo; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Parameter reasoning, 25; model interpretation, 25; scenario analysis, 20; validation and model-risk discussion, 20; presentation and reproducibility, 10.

7.2 Project 14: Why Hull–White Must Fit Today’s Curve

Scenario. A colleague proposes using a historically estimated equilibrium model to price a callable bond without first matching current market prices.

Objectives

  • Explain the difference between equilibrium and no-arbitrage models.
  • Demonstrate why fitting today’s discount curve matters for time-0 valuation.
  • Show qualitatively how mean reversion and volatility affect future rate states and option value.

Data sources. Current discount factors or zero rates from U.S. Treasury, FRED, or Bank of Canada data; a small instructor-provided Hull–White tree or calibration output; public swaption or cap-volatility commentary if available.

Deliverables. A model-comparison table; a curve-fit or repricing exhibit; two parameter-sensitivity scenarios; a recommendation for plain versus option-embedded bonds; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Model distinction, 25; curve-fit reasoning, 25; sensitivity interpretation, 20; instrument-specific recommendation, 20; presentation and attribution, 10.

8 Lecture 8: Embedded Options and Option-Adjusted Measures

8.1 Project 15: Callable Bond Relative-Value Review

Scenario. A portfolio manager must choose between a callable corporate bond and an option-free or weakly callable alternative from a comparable issuer.

Objectives

  • Compare yield to worst, static spread, call schedule, and reinvestment risk.
  • Decompose the callable bond conceptually into an option-free bond minus the issuer’s call option.
  • Evaluate rate and volatility scenarios without treating the larger quoted spread as free compensation.

Data sources. FINRA Fixed Income Data/TRACE; issuer prospectus supplements from SEC EDGAR or SEDAR+; benchmark curves from FRED, the U.S. Treasury, or the Bank of Canada; instructor-provided OAS snapshots if needed.

Deliverables. A matched-bond term sheet; call timeline; yield/spread comparison; three rate-volatility scenarios; a mandate-based recommendation; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Bond and call-term accuracy, 20; spread and yield analysis, 25; optionality reasoning, 30; recommendation and caveats, 15; presentation and sourcing, 10.

8.2 Project 16: Backward Induction in an Interest-Rate Tree

Scenario. A valuation committee wants an auditable explanation of how callability or putability changes a bond’s value.

Objectives

  • Price an option-free and callable or putable bond in a small recombining tree.
  • Apply the exercise rule at each eligible node and calculate embedded-option value.
  • Test sensitivity to volatility, call or put price, and spread.

Data sources. Instructor-provided short-rate tree and security terms; a same-date benchmark curve from the U.S. Treasury or Bank of Canada for context; issuer filings for a realistic exercise schedule.

Deliverables. A node-by-node spreadsheet; option-free and option-adjusted values; exercise-node map; at least three sensitivities; an audit note explaining timing conventions; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Tree and cash-flow setup, 20; backward-induction accuracy, 30; exercise logic, 20; sensitivity and interpretation, 20; presentation and auditability, 10.

9 Lecture 9: Interest-Rate Futures

9.1 Project 17: Hedge a Bond Position with Treasury Futures

Scenario. A portfolio manager wants to reduce the rate exposure of a Treasury or high-quality corporate bond position for one month without selling the bonds.

Objectives

  • Estimate the position’s dollar duration or DV01.
  • Select an appropriate Treasury futures contract and calculate a hedge ratio.
  • Explain conversion factors, cheapest-to-deliver uncertainty, basis risk, and hedge slippage.

Data sources. CME Group Treasury futures contract specifications, conversion factors, and settlement data; U.S. Treasury yields and security terms; FINRA data for a corporate cash position; instructor-provided conversion-factor and cheapest-to-deliver snapshots.

Deliverables. Cash-position risk measure; contract choice; hedge-ratio calculation; two nonparallel-rate scenarios; implementation and basis-risk note; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Cash-position risk measurement, 20; futures selection and hedge ratio, 30; delivery and basis-risk analysis, 25; scenario interpretation, 15; presentation and sources, 10.

9.2 Project 18: Read the SOFR Futures Curve

Scenario. A macro fixed-income desk needs a briefing on the policy path implied by the SOFR futures strip and how that view changed around a central-bank announcement.

Objectives

  • Convert selected SOFR futures prices into implied forward rates.
  • Compare strips on two dates and distinguish timing from magnitude of expected policy changes.
  • Explain why futures-implied rates are not pure forecasts.

Data sources. CME Group Three-Month SOFR futures settlements and contract specifications; Federal Reserve policy statements and meeting calendar; effective federal funds rate from FRED; instructor-provided settlement snapshots if historical access is limited.

Deliverables. Two implied-rate strips; contract-month change table; policy-event timeline; interpretation of term or risk premiums and limitations; a desk conclusion; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Contract and price conversion accuracy, 25; curve-change analysis, 25; policy interpretation, 20; futures-versus-forecast caveats, 20; presentation and reproducibility, 10.

10 Lecture 10: Credit Spread Exposure and Credit Risk Modeling

10.1 Project 19: Spread Duration and Credit Scenario Budget

Scenario. A credit portfolio has a yield advantage over government bonds, but the investment committee wants to know how much spread widening would erase one year of carry.

Objectives

  • Estimate spread duration and annual carry for a bond or small portfolio.
  • Calculate break-even spread widening and scenario losses.
  • Distinguish spread risk from default loss, Treasury-rate risk, and liquidity risk.

Data sources. FINRA Fixed Income Data/TRACE and issuer filings; option-adjusted spread indexes from FRED; matched Treasury data from the U.S. Treasury; instructor-provided bond analytics where OAS is not freely available.

Deliverables. Position and benchmark profile; carry and spread-duration calculations; break-even widening; mild, severe, and recession scenarios; a risk-budget recommendation; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Input and convention quality, 20; carry and break-even calculations, 30; scenario construction, 20; risk decomposition and recommendation, 20; presentation and documentation, 10.

10.2 Project 20: Expected Loss and Bondholder Recovery

Scenario. A lender is comparing two speculative-grade bonds with different default probabilities, seniority, collateral, and prices.

Objectives

  • Estimate simple one-year or cumulative expected credit loss using transparent assumptions.
  • Analyze how seniority, collateral, covenants, and capital structure affect recovery.
  • Compare expected loss with the observed spread while recognizing liquidity and risk premiums.

Data sources. Issuer capital-structure and covenant disclosures from SEC EDGAR or SEDAR+; bond prices and trades from FINRA; public default and recovery studies from rating agencies; FRED high-yield spread indexes; instructor-provided default tables if reports are paywalled.

Deliverables. Capital-structure diagram; default-probability and recovery assumptions; expected-loss scenarios; spread-versus-loss comparison; a creditor preference with limitations; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Capital-structure research, 20; expected-loss calculations, 25; recovery reasoning, 25; spread comparison and judgment, 20; presentation and citations, 10.

11 Lecture 11: Interest-Rate Options

11.1 Project 21: Cap, Floor, or Swap for a Borrower?

Scenario. A floating-rate borrower wants protection against rising rates but is uncertain whether to buy a cap, enter a fixed-for-floating swap, or remain unhedged.

Objectives

  • Compare payoff asymmetry, premium, opportunity cost, and accounting or liquidity considerations.
  • Evaluate the alternatives under at least four future-rate scenarios.
  • Recommend a hedge consistent with a stated risk tolerance and budget.

Data sources. CME Group SOFR futures and options information; current or instructor-provided cap and swap quote snapshots; SOFR from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York or FRED; a public company’s floating-rate debt disclosure for realistic exposure terms.

Deliverables. Exposure profile; cap, swap, and unhedged payoff table; rate-scenario cost comparison; qualitative volatility discussion; a hedge recommendation and trigger; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Exposure and instrument setup, 20; payoff and scenario accuracy, 30; option-versus-swap reasoning, 20; recommendation and risk tradeoffs, 20; presentation and source note, 10.

11.2 Project 22: Option on Treasury Futures Event Hedge

Scenario. A bond manager expects substantial rate volatility around a policy or inflation release but wants to limit downside without giving up all upside.

Objectives

  • Choose a call, put, or simple option spread on a Treasury futures contract.
  • Map the option payoff to the manager’s underlying duration exposure.
  • Compare the option hedge with a linear futures hedge across price and yield scenarios.

Data sources. CME Group Treasury futures and options contract specifications, settlements, and delayed quotes; economic-release calendars from the Federal Reserve, BLS, or Bank of Canada; instructor-provided option-chain snapshots where historical chains are unavailable.

Deliverables. Exposure and event definition; contract and strike selection; expiration payoff diagram; premium-inclusive scenario table; comparison with a futures hedge; post-event evaluation plan; and a 10–12 minute team presentation.

Rubric (100 points). Event and exposure framing, 15; contract and strike selection, 25; payoff and premium calculations, 25; futures comparison and hedge judgment, 25; presentation and reproducibility, 10.
