Learning Objectives

After completing this demo you should be able to decompose corporate-bond risk into Treasury-rate, spread, default, and liquidity components; calculate spread duration and duration times spread (DTS); compare spread carry with widening risk; and evaluate mild and severe portfolio scenarios.

1 Inputs and Assumptions

The decision: a credit manager is offered more spread by moving into a lower-rated bond. Does the extra carry justify the loss if spreads widen? Follow the chain position → dollar sensitivity → carry cushion → stress loss. For each step, predict the sign before opening the folded calculation.

All values are illustrative, market-like inputs, not current quotes. The portfolio is intentionally small enough to audit by hand. Prices are near par; annual carry is approximated by spread times market value; spread duration is treated as constant; Treasury and spread P&L use first-order duration; PDs are physical one-year probabilities; defaults and mark-to-market spread changes are reported separately to avoid double counting.

2 Treasury and Spread Decomposition

For small changes,

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P}\approx-D_T\Delta y_T-D_s\Delta s. \]

Treasury duration measures exposure to the benchmark curve. Spread duration measures exposure to the bond’s compensation over that curve. DTS rewrites spread risk for a proportional spread shock:

\[ \text{DTS}=D_s s, \qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P}\approx-\text{DTS}\frac{\Delta s}{s}. \]

Equal market values do not imply equal credit risk. Wider-spread bonds can contribute disproportionately to DTS even when their spread duration is shorter.

3 Carry Versus Widening Breakeven

NoteAudit the utility position

The utility’s $12 million market value and spread duration of 4 give $12,000,000 × 4 × 0.0001 = $4,800 per bp of spread risk. A 25 bp widening therefore costs approximately $120,000. Its 78 bp annual spread carry is only $93,600. Even a small-looking spread move can erase a year’s spread income; spread is quoted per year, but a price loss can occur today.

Approximate annual spread carry is \(MV\times s\). The spread widening that consumes one year of spread carry is

\[ \Delta s_{BE}\approx\frac{s}{D_s}. \]

This is a mark-to-market breakeven, not a total-return forecast. It ignores rolldown, Treasury carry, default, taxes, and financing.

Expected credit loss is

\[ EL=PD\times LGD\times EAD, \qquad LGD=1-\text{Recovery Rate}. \]

Spread is not expected loss. It may also compensate for systematic credit risk, uncertainty, liquidity, taxes, and supply-demand effects.

Show calculation 
bonds["LGD"] = 1 - bonds["Recovery rate"]
bonds["Expected loss ($)"] = (
    bonds["Market value ($mm)"] * 1_000_000 * bonds["One-year PD"] * bonds["LGD"]
)
bonds["Liquidity reserve ($)"] = (
    bonds["Market value ($mm)"] * 1_000_000 * bonds["Liquidity reserve (bp/year)"] / 10_000
)
bonds["Carry after EL and liquidity ($)"] = (
    bonds["Annual spread carry ($)"]
    - bonds["Expected loss ($)"]

5 Mild and Severe Scenarios

The scenarios distinguish a benchmark-rate shock from issuer spread shocks. Expected loss is shown as a separate one-year planning measure rather than added to scenario P&L.

Show calculation 
scenarios = {
    "Mild slowdown": {
        "Treasury shock (bp)": -20,
        "Spread shocks (bp)": np.array([15, 30, 40, 80]),
    },
    "Severe recession": {
        "Treasury shock (bp)": -75,
        "Spread shocks (bp)": np.array([55, 110, 160, 350]),
    },
}

rows = []
for name, scenario in scenarios.items():
    rate_pnl = -bonds["Treasury DV01 ($/bp)"] * scenario["Treasury shock (bp)"]
    spread_pnl = -bonds["Spread DV01 ($/bp)"] * scenario["Spread shocks (bp)"]
    rows.append({
        "Scenario": name,
        "Treasury P&L ($)": rate_pnl.sum(),
        "Spread P&L ($)": spread_pnl.sum(),
        "Total mark-to-market P&L ($)": rate_pnl.sum() + spread_pnl.sum(),
    })

scenario_table = pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("Scenario")
scenario_table.round(0)

6 Why the Risks Differ

  • Rate risk is exposure to the benchmark Treasury curve and can often be hedged with Treasury futures or swaps.
  • Spread risk is mark-to-market exposure to changing required compensation for credit and liquidity risk.
  • Default risk is a discrete credit event with uncertain recovery; PD and LGD describe expected loss, not daily spread volatility.
  • Liquidity risk is the cost or inability to trade near an observed price, often worst when spreads are already widening.

8 Financial Interpretation

A wide spread is not automatically attractive. The analyst should ask how much of the spread may be consumed by expected loss and liquidity, how much widening erases carry, and whether falling Treasury yields are likely to offset spread losses in stress. DTS is useful for proportional spread shocks; DV01 is useful for basis-point shocks; neither replaces explicit default and liquidity analysis.

9 Guided decision and market handoff

  1. Hold Treasury yields fixed. Compare a 25 bp widening in every bond with a 25% increase in each bond’s spread. DV01 ranks the first exposure; DTS addresses the second. The shocks are not interchangeable.
  2. Allow a Treasury rally. Use the severe scenario to explain why government yields falling does not guarantee a gain on a corporate bond.
  3. Write the recommendation. Cite carry breakeven, spread-loss concentration, and the assumption about default. Do not add expected default loss to the mark-to-market stress as though they were independent realized losses.

Inspect FINRA’s security and trade lookup for an issuer’s terms and trade history. Record the benchmark, timestamp, callable status, and spread definition. A yield difference, a Z-spread, and an OAS are not interchangeable inputs. Keep the embedded four-bond case if a comparable security or permitted dataset is unavailable; the supplied PDs are assumptions, not ratings-agency estimates.

```{python}
def readable_table(frame):
    frame = frame.copy()
    if len(frame.columns) and frame.iloc[:, 0].dtype == object:
        frame = frame.set_index(frame.columns[0])
    if len(frame.columns) > 6 and len(frame) <= 4:
        frame = frame.T
    panels = []
    for start in range(0, len(frame.columns), 3):
        part = frame.iloc[:, start:start+3]
        markup = part.to_html(border=0, na_rep="", float_format=lambda v: f"{v:,.4f}".rstrip("0").rstrip("."))
```

```{python}
    "Portfolio DTS": portfolio_dts,
}).round(4)
```

Equal market values do not imply equal credit risk. Wider-spread bonds can contribute disproportionately to DTS even when their spread duration is shorter.

## Carry Versus Widening Breakeven

::: {.callout-note}
## Audit the utility position

The utility's $12 million market value and spread duration of 4 give
$12,000,000 × 4 × 0.0001 = **$4,800 per bp** of spread risk.
A 25 bp widening therefore costs approximately $120,000. Its 78 bp annual
spread carry is only $93,600. Even a small-looking spread move can erase a
year's spread income; spread is quoted per year, but a price loss can occur today.
:::

```{python}

    "Capital weight": 100 * bonds["Market value ($mm)"] / bonds["Market value ($mm)"].sum(),

```

Approximate annual spread carry is $MV\times s$. The spread widening that consumes one year of spread carry is

$$
\Delta s_{BE}\approx\frac{s}{D_s}.
$$

This is a mark-to-market breakeven, not a total-return forecast. It ignores rolldown, Treasury carry, default, taxes, and financing.

```{python}
#| label: carry-breakeven
bonds["Annual spread carry ($)"] = (
    bonds["Market value ($mm)"] * 1_000_000 * bonds["Spread (bp)"] / 10_000
)
bonds["Widening breakeven (bp)"] = bonds["Spread (bp)"] / bonds["Spread duration"]
bonds[["Bond", "Annual spread carry ($)", "Widening breakeven (bp)"]].round(1)
```

## Expected Loss and Liquidity Reserve

```{python}

#| fig-alt: "Four declining lines show annual spread carry less mark-to-market loss as spreads widen."
widening_grid = np.arange(0, 161, 5)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.6))
for i, bond in bonds.iterrows():
    cushion = bond["Spread (bp)"] - bond["Spread duration"] * widening_grid
    ax.plot(widening_grid, cushion, linestyle=["-", "--", "-.", ":"][i], label=bond["Bond"])
ax.axhline(0, color="#333333", linewidth=1)

ax.legend(fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Expected credit loss is

$$
EL=PD\times LGD\times EAD,
\qquad LGD=1-\text{Recovery Rate}.
$$

Spread is not expected loss. It may also compensate for systematic credit risk, uncertainty, liquidity, taxes, and supply-demand effects.

```{python}
#| label: expected-loss
bonds["LGD"] = 1 - bonds["Recovery rate"]
bonds["Expected loss ($)"] = (
    bonds["Market value ($mm)"] * 1_000_000 * bonds["One-year PD"] * bonds["LGD"]
)
bonds["Liquidity reserve ($)"] = (
    bonds["Market value ($mm)"] * 1_000_000 * bonds["Liquidity reserve (bp/year)"] / 10_000
)
bonds["Carry after EL and liquidity ($)"] = (
    bonds["Annual spread carry ($)"]
    - bonds["Expected loss ($)"]
    - bonds["Liquidity reserve ($)"]
)
bonds[["Bond", "Annual spread carry ($)", "Expected loss ($)",
       "Liquidity reserve ($)", "Carry after EL and liquidity ($)"]].round(0)
```

## Mild and Severe Scenarios

The scenarios distinguish a benchmark-rate shock from issuer spread shocks. Expected loss is shown as a separate one-year planning measure rather than added to scenario P&L.

```{python}
#| label: scenarios
scenarios = {
    "Mild slowdown": {
        "Treasury shock (bp)": -20,
        "Spread shocks (bp)": np.array([15, 30, 40, 80]),
    },
    "Severe recession": {
        "Treasury shock (bp)": -75,
        "Spread shocks (bp)": np.array([55, 110, 160, 350]),
    },
}

rows = []
for name, scenario in scenarios.items():
    rate_pnl = -bonds["Treasury DV01 ($/bp)"] * scenario["Treasury shock (bp)"]
    spread_pnl = -bonds["Spread DV01 ($/bp)"] * scenario["Spread shocks (bp)"]
    rows.append({
        "Scenario": name,
        "Treasury P&L ($)": rate_pnl.sum(),
        "Spread P&L ($)": spread_pnl.sum(),
        "Total mark-to-market P&L ($)": rate_pnl.sum() + spread_pnl.sum(),
    })

scenario_table = pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("Scenario")
scenario_table.round(0)
```

## Why the Risks Differ

- **Rate risk** is exposure to the benchmark Treasury curve and can often be hedged with Treasury futures or swaps.
- **Spread risk** is mark-to-market exposure to changing required compensation for credit and liquidity risk.
- **Default risk** is a discrete credit event with uncertain recovery; PD and LGD describe expected loss, not daily spread volatility.
- **Liquidity risk** is the cost or inability to trade near an observed price, often worst when spreads are already widening.

## Financial Interpretation

A wide spread is not automatically attractive. The analyst should ask how much of the spread may be consumed by expected loss and liquidity, how much widening erases carry, and whether falling Treasury yields are likely to offset spread losses in stress. DTS is useful for proportional spread shocks; DV01 is useful for basis-point shocks; neither replaces explicit default and liquidity analysis.

## Guided decision and market handoff

1. **Hold Treasury yields fixed.** Compare a 25 bp widening in every bond with
   a 25% increase in each bond's spread. DV01 ranks the first exposure; DTS
   addresses the second. The shocks are not interchangeable.
2. **Allow a Treasury rally.** Use the severe scenario to explain why government
   yields falling does not guarantee a gain on a corporate bond.
3. **Write the recommendation.** Cite carry breakeven, spread-loss concentration,
   and the assumption about default. Do not add expected default loss to the
   mark-to-market stress as though they were independent realized losses.

Inspect [FINRA's security and trade lookup](https://www.finra.org/finra-data/fixed-income)
for an issuer's terms and trade history. Record the benchmark, timestamp, callable
status, and spread definition. A yield difference, a Z-spread, and an OAS are not
interchangeable inputs. Keep the embedded four-bond case if a comparable security
or permitted dataset is unavailable; the supplied PDs are assumptions, not ratings-agency estimates.
